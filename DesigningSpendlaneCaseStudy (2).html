02 About This Project

A practice project, designed with the same rigor as client work.

Spendlane isn't a client engagement. There's no company behind it, no funding round, no real users yet. I built it because most of my professional work lives in enterprise operator consoles and internal admin tools, and I wanted to stretch a different muscle: consumer-adjacent fintech UX, where trust, clarity and speed all have to hold up in the same screen.

Everything on this page, the flows, the screens, the copy, is my own design work, built from scratch in Figma and then recreated in code for this write-up. I'm sharing it the way I'd share any case study: the problem I set myself, the decisions I made, and what I'd test next if this were real.

Why this project

To design a full fintech product surface end to end, from a blank page to a working set of screens, without a spec to inherit.

What I controlled

Problem framing, information architecture, visual system, and every screen, all designed and built solo.

What's next

Usability testing with real finance admins is the obvious next step, see the Reflection section for specifics.

03 The Problem

Small teams find out they've overspent after it's too late to do anything about it.

Once a company has more than one card-holder, spend visibility usually breaks. Admins reconcile card statements after the fact instead of watching budgets in real time. Employees wait days for a manual approval because there's no shared context for the person approving it. And when someone leaves or changes teams, nobody remembers to freeze their card.

Reactive, not live

Budgets are tracked in spreadsheets after the statement arrives, not while the spending is happening.

Slow approvals

Requests sit in someone's inbox with no budget context, so approving feels like a guess, not a decision.

No card-level control

One shared card, or cards nobody can freeze or adjust individually when circumstances change.

04 Goals & Approach

Three principles instead of one long feature list.

Rather than list every screen upfront, I set three principles and let them drive every layout and copy decision below.

01 · Status at a glance

Budget health should be readable in under two seconds, before an admin clicks into anything.

02 · Context travels with the request

An approval should carry enough budget context that saying yes or no is a fast, confident call.

03 · Control lives at the card

Freezing a card or adjusting a limit should be a one-click action, not a support ticket.

16 Design System

A small, deliberate palette.

Spendlane's brand color is teal, not the red or blue most fintech products default to. I wanted a color that could carry "positive, on track" states without ever being mistaken for a warning, freeing red for the one thing it should mean: something needs attention now.

Teal = healthy

Used for on-track budgets, active cards, and primary actions. Never used for anything that needs attention.

Amber = watch this

A budget crossing 70% or a large single transaction, a nudge before it becomes a problem.

Red = act now

Reserved for declined transactions and over-limit states, so it's rare enough to still mean something.

17 Reflection
"Designing without a client also means designing without a stakeholder to disagree with me, which is exactly why I want to test it with real people before I'd call any of it validated.

If I carried this further, three things would come first: usability testing the approval flow with actual finance admins to see if "budget context in one sentence" is actually enough context to decide on, a mobile approval flow since most of these decisions happen away from a desk, and a notifications system so an admin isn't relying on remembering to check the dashboard.

What I'd validate first

Whether the one-line budget context on the Approvals screen is genuinely enough for a confident decision, or whether admins want a link to the full ledger.

What I'd build next

A mobile approval flow, since the research pattern I've seen elsewhere suggests most approvals happen away from a desk.

What surprised me

How much of "trust" in a fintech screen comes from restraint, saying less, more clearly, rather than showing every number available.
