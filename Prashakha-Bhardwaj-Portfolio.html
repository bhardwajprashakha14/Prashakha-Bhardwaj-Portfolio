03 Case Study 01

Designing Channel Creation: From a One-Line Requirement to 150 Fields

ClientEnterprise OTT Platform
RoleUX Designer, UX Architect, UI Designer
PlatformOperator Console (Web)
DurationMulti-Release, Ongoing
Overview

Channel Creation began as a single line of requirement, "we need channel creation," with no field list, no wireframe, and no spec to inherit. Working session by session with a Solution Developer, Solution Architect and Senior Developer, we recognised close to 150 fields the platform actually needed, spanning general metadata, delivery, security, enablers and advertisement, plus entire sub-features like Bouquet Management and Catalogue linking that surfaced along the way.

As UX Designer, UX Architect and UI Designer, I turned that discovery into a validated, multi-section stepper with a mandatory Preview before submit, then reused the same pattern for Update, safe Deletion, and a Recording feature added long after the original launch.

The Challenge
  • ~150 fields cannot live in a single form. Scrolling replaces scanning, and abandonment climbs the moment users lose their place.
  • Because a channel is business-critical, operators needed real confidence before submitting, not a chance to fix mistakes after the fact.
  • Deleting a channel unsafely could orphan live recordings for millions of active viewers if the sequencing was not enforced by the UI itself.
Process
01
Field Discovery

Working sessions with Solution Architecture and Engineering to recognise and name ~150 real fields.

02
Taxonomy & IA

Grouped every field into General, Delivery, Security, Enablers and Advertisement sections.

03
Stepper & Validation

Built a step-by-step flow where Next stays disabled until mandatory fields in that step are complete.

04
Preview & Confirm

Added a mandatory review step plus explicit confirmation before any channel is created.

05
Reuse & Extend

Carried the same pattern into Update, safe Deletion, and the later Recording feature.

Outcome
~150 fields
Structured, not scattered

Every field grouped into five ownable sections instead of one overwhelming form.

1 pattern
Every lifecycle stage

Create, Update, Delete and Recording all reuse the same stepper and Preview model.

12+
Iterations, zero redesigns

New requirements kept landing across releases without breaking the core pattern.

04 Case Study 02

Redesigning the Enterprise Audit Experience

ClientEnterprise OTT Platform
RoleUI/UX Designer, end to end
PlatformOperator Console (Web)
Duration4-6 Weeks
Overview

Every action inside the operator console, publishing a channel, updating a VOD asset, changing a household's entitlement, is recorded as an audit log. But audit history lived in a completely separate module, disconnected from the entity it described, so investigating a change meant leaving the record you were looking at to go find it somewhere else.

I led the redesign that moved Version History directly into the entity itself, and introduced a side-by-side Version Comparison view that replaced manual, field-by-field inspection with color-coded, at-a-glance differencing.

The Challenge
  • Investigating a single change meant leaving the entity, remembering its Entity ID, and rebuilding filters from memory before a single result appeared.
  • Comparing two versions meant opening two separate audit records and manually holding both in your head. There was no comparison feature at all.
  • This eleven-step investigation was the single most time-consuming, most frequently repeated task for the operations team.
Process
01
Discovery

Stakeholder discussions, support-team shadowing and developer workshops surfaced that operators never start from the Audit module. They need history while already viewing an entity.

02
Heuristic Audit

Evaluated the existing flow against usability heuristics. Recognition over recall and information hierarchy scored lowest.

03
IA & Flow Redesign

Moved Version History into Entity Details, collapsing an 11-step investigation into 6 steps.

04
Wireframing

Explored horizontal cards, a modal, and a full-page comparison before settling on an in-context History and Compare pattern.

05
Visual & Interaction

Designed the Added, Updated, Removed, Unchanged comparison system, with Compare disabled until exactly two versions are selected.

Outcome
11 to 6
Investigation steps

Cut nearly in half by removing every unnecessary module switch.

0
Manual memorization

Manual field-by-field comparison replaced with instant, color-coded differencing.

1 pattern
Reusable across entities

The same Version History and Compare pattern was designed to scale across Channels, VOD, Households and Catalogs.

05 Case Study 03

Designing the Operator's Control Room for Enterprise OTT

ClientEnterprise OTT Platform
RoleUI/UX Designer, end to end
PlatformWeb, Mobile, STB, Smart TV, DTH
DurationJun 2024 to Present
Overview

The operator console is the system pay-TV and OTT operators use to run their business: publishing content, controlling who has access to what, provisioning devices, and configuring the platform itself. It is built for admins, not consumers, but the stakes are consumer-facing. A misconfigured entitlement can lock a paying subscriber out of content they own.

I own UX end to end here, from the enterprise admin console down to the device-side screens it ultimately controls, across STB, Mobile, Tablet, Smart TV, and OTT. Two specific modules from this engagement are broken out in full detail as Case Study 01 ↑ (Channel Creation) and Case Study 02 ↑ (Version History and Comparison).

The Challenge

The console had grown to 20+ modules, each carrying its own business logic and role-based permission rules. That growth created three compounding problems for the people who used it every day.

  • High cognitive load: dense data tables and permission-gated actions with little consistency in how they were presented module to module.
  • Steep onboarding: new admin users struggled to build a mental model of where an action lived and who was allowed to take it.
  • Slow, repetitive design-to-dev handoff: similar patterns (status indicators, permission gates, action tables) were being redesigned from scratch for every new module.
Process
01
Discovery

Unpacked entitlement models and RBAC matrices with Product and Engineering. Audited existing modules for inconsistency.

02
IA

Re-grouped navigation around admin mental models (content, entitlement, device, configuration) instead of backend architecture.

03
Design System

Built reusable, sprint-ready components for tables, permission-gated actions and status states, adopted across 20+ modules.

04
Prototyping

Interactive Figma prototypes for complex flows: content publishing, entitlement assignment, device provisioning.

05
Usability Testing

Iterative rounds with admin users to validate and refine task flows before dev handoff.

Device-Side Design

Beyond the console, I designed the end-user-facing screens those admin decisions ultimately drive, adapting the same underlying patterns for remote-control navigation on STB and Smart TV, and touch interaction on Mobile and Tablet, so an entitlement or content rule looks and behaves consistently no matter where a subscriber encounters it.

Outcome
20+
Modules unified

Complex business rules and permissions translated into structured, consistent UX flows across admin dashboards.

↑
Task completion

Iterative usability testing across admin dashboards improved completion rates release over release.

↓
Design-to-dev time

Reusable design system components cut redundant back-and-forth across 20+ modules.

06 Case Study 04

Getting People from "I Need a Ride" to Booked, Fast

ClientDogma Systems India
RoleUI/UX Designer
PlatformWeb & Mobile
DurationAug 2022 to Jun 2024
Overview

Dogma Systems builds transport and vehicle rental products: self-service booking apps for riders (scooters, self-drive cars) and the CRM/rental-management tools operators use behind the scenes. I designed across both sides of that marketplace, including a scooter self-booking app, a self-drive rental app, and the operator-facing rental CRM.

The Challenge

Two very different users, two very different pressures. Riders wanted to go from "I need a vehicle" to booked in as few taps as possible, with real-time confidence in availability. Rental operators needed a CRM to manage fleets, bookings and logistics without a training manual, and many were juggling it alongside phone and walk-in bookings.

Process
01
User Interviews

Talked to end users and operators to ground feature decisions in real booking friction, not assumptions.

02
Personas & Analysis

Benchmarked against ride-share and rental apps to prioritize what actually mattered.

03
Information Architecture

Simplified the booking flow and the CRM sitemap so both sides of the product stayed legible.

04
Responsive Prototypes

Mobile-first booking flows and responsive web CRM dashboards for operators.

05
Handoff

Detailed design specs partnered with developers and QA for accurate implementation.

Outcome
3
Products shipped

Scooter self-booking, self-drive rental, and CRM tooling all shipped with responsive, user-tested flows.

↓
Booking friction

Streamlined browse, select, confirm, pick-up flow reduced steps to complete a booking.

↓
Rework

Close developer and QA partnership meant user flows shipped accurately, with minimal rework.

07 Case Study 05

Rebuilding Trust in a Product's Design Process

ClientACIL Technologies
RoleUI/UX Designer
PlatformWeb (Admin Panels)
DurationDec 2020 to Jul 2022
Overview

ACIL's internal dashboards and admin panels were used daily by internal teams and client stakeholders, but recurring UX pain points, flagged repeatedly through client feedback, had quietly eroded confidence in the design process itself.

The Challenge

The problem was not a single broken screen. It was a pattern: confusing task flows and unclear information hierarchy kept resurfacing across release cycles, and stakeholders had started to expect it rather than report it.

Process
01
Sitemap Audit

Defined clear sitemap structures and applied consistent UX best practices across interfaces.

02
Usability Testing

Ran testing across release cycles rather than once, to catch drift early.

03
Pain-Point Triage

Took client-reported issues directly into the design backlog, resolved and shipped visibly.

04
Iteration

Refined task flows release over release based on what testing and feedback actually showed.

Outcome
↑
Completion rates

Task flows and completion rates improved across release cycles.

Restored
Stakeholder trust

Visibly resolving recurring pain points rebuilt confidence in the design process.

Cleaner
Information architecture

Consistent sitemap structure reduced confusion across the product's admin surfaces.
