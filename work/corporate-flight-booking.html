Case study 04 Corporate travel · Design challenge

Policy aware corporate flight booking

For a work trip, the right flight is not always the cheapest or fastest. This flow shows policy, trip readiness and document checks on each flight while people compare, and keeps requesting approval clearly separate from booking.

RoleSolo UI/UX designer
TypeDesign challenge
PlatformsDesktop 1440, mobile 390
StatusConcept
Reading timeAbout 5 min

NoteVoyalo is a placeholder brand and all flight data comes from the challenge brief.

Summary for reviewers

Product and users
A corporate flight booking flow. Employees booking work trips, and the managers who approve them.
Problem
For a work trip, the cheapest or fastest flight can still be wrong. It may break policy, need approval, or use a route the traveller's documents don't cover. Most flows reveal this only at checkout.
My ownership
Solo: flows, four core screens on desktop and mobile, states, a semantic risk system and a component library.
Team
Solo, from a design challenge brief.
Main constraint
Fixed brief: one traveller, one trip, five imperfect options. Flight data came from the brief and Voyalo is a placeholder brand.
Period and status
Design challenge. Concept, not built or tested.
What I designed
Search results that show policy, trip readiness and document checks on each card, a review step that previews what the manager receives, and states for when things go wrong.
Evidence and limits
No research participants. "Readable in 3 to 5 seconds" is a design target, not a measurement. The readiness label is a hypothesis to test.

01 The brief

One traveller, one trip, five imperfect options.

The brief asked for four screens for an international work trip: search results, flight details, traveller details, and review and approval, on desktop and mobile, plus one new idea of my own.

Riya, a senior account manager, needs to fly from Delhi to London. She is booking 12 days ahead, but policy asks for 14. So every option needs approval, even the ones that follow every other rule.

02 The problem

Different risks, often shown the same way.

Problem

Policy shows up too late

Approval needs usually appear at checkout, after the traveller has already chosen.

Problem

Different risks look alike

A policy exception, a long layover and a missing transit visa are different problems, yet often share one red label.

Problem

Managers lack context

Approvers see a fare and a reason but not the trade offs, so requests go back and forth.

How might we help employees pick a sensible flight that fits policy, and give their manager what they need to approve it at a glance?
03 Three questions

Policy, readiness and documents are separate questions.

The key structural decision was to stop treating "is this flight OK?" as one question. Each card answers three, in its own place and words.

Three separate questions on each flight
QuestionWhat it asksWhere it shows
Company policyDoes this fare follow the rules, and does it need approval?On every card, and in the review step with a rule by rule table
Trip readinessIs this itinerary practical for a work trip: connections, arrival time, layovers?A rule based label with its reasons, on cards and in a detail sheet
Document checkDo the traveller's passport and visas cover this route?On cards when there is a problem, and on the traveller step
In the design
Approval is not booking

Requesting approval never looks like a confirmed booking. The flow reads You → Manager → Booking confirmation, and the review step says the trip isn't booked until it is approved.

Design target
"Readable in 3 to 5 seconds"

This was the target I designed the card order for. I haven't measured it with people.

The semantic system behind the labels
04 Details to approval

Send once, with what the manager needs.

Limitation
What I didn't design

I designed the traveller's preview of the manager's request, not the manager's own approval screen. That is the next screen I would design.

The same flow, rearranged for a small screen.

06 New idea

Business Trip Readiness.

Price and duration don't tell you whether an international work trip is practical. Readiness gives each itinerary one of four labels, Excellent, Fair, Needs attention or Not travel ready, from clear rules, and always lists its reasons.

Assumption
Still a hypothesis

I would test whether travellers trust a rule based label, and whether showing reasons is enough for them to act on it.

07 States and system

When something fails, nothing is lost.

Design system for handoff
08 Reflection

What I would test next.

Next I would test with travellers and approvers: do people trust the readiness label, does separating the three questions speed up choosing, and does the manager preview reduce back and forth? Then I would design the manager's approval screen.
